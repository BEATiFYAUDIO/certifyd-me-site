# Digital Music News Shows Why Accurate Metadata Still May Not Get You Paid

Digital Music News recently published an Equalizer Consulting article on common metadata failures that can block music royalties. Its clearest example is not a missing title or a typo: an artist’s entire catalog appeared to belong to another artist with the same name, even though the work- and recording-level details were accurate.

That is a useful warning for anyone who thinks clean release data automatically means clean payment data. A catalog can look correct while the connection between the work and the person entitled to it is wrong.

## Accurate fields are not enough

The article describes a case of mistaken identity in which titles, release dates and identifiers were all in order. But a database had linked the artist’s discography to someone else, and that error spread to systems used for online search.

The same basic weakness can appear on the publishing side. The article also describes a songwriter whose registered works were crossed with another songwriter sharing the same name. A valid identifier can still be attached to the wrong person if the workflow does not catch the mismatch.

## Metadata is a payment dependency

The obvious lesson is to take metadata seriously. The deeper lesson is that music data is not only descriptive. It helps determine who is connected to a work when rights, revenue and recognition move through different systems.

That changes the question creators should ask. It is not simply, “Are my release fields filled in?” It is, “Can the systems handling my music consistently connect this work to me?” Names, unique identity details and the links between recording and publishing records need to hold up as a catalog moves across databases.

## Catalog context should not live only in disconnected systems

A mistaken identity link can sit inside a database that the creator does not operate, then affect search and royalty workflows elsewhere. When the work, release and creator context exists only as separate entries across third-party systems, creators lack a creator-controlled foundation for the context behind their own catalog.

## A practical response

Creators and small music businesses should treat identity checks as part of release and catalog maintenance, not an occasional cleanup project. Review how artist and songwriter names appear across recordings and works, keep unique identity details consistent, and investigate quickly when a catalog is attributed to someone else.

The problem described by Digital Music News is easy to miss because the familiar metadata can look fine. But payment depends on more than accurate-looking fields. It depends on whether the right person remains explicitly connected to the music before value starts moving.
