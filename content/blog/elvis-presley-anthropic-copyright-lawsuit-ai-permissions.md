# Elvis Presley Rightsholders’ Anthropic Lawsuit Puts AI Permissions at the Center

Elvis Presley music rightsholders have sued Anthropic, alleging that 274 Presley-associated songs were used to train AI models without permission or a license. Digital Music News reported on October 26, 2026, that the plaintiffs include a subsidiary of Authentic Brands Group and other companies with interests in Presley’s music. The complaint also alleges that Anthropic’s models can generate unauthorized versions of existing songs.

The songs named in the report include “All Shook Up,” “Can’t Help Falling in Love,” and “A Little Less Conversation.” The plaintiffs are seeking statutory damages of up to $150,000 per allegedly infringed song—about $41.1 million in total, according to the report. Anthropic has maintained that training AI models on copyrighted material can and should be protected by U.S. fair use law.

## Permission cannot be an afterthought

The immediate issue is familiar: whether a company can train a model on copyrighted music without a negotiated authorization. But the case points to a tougher operating question. If a music-capable AI product gains value from works at ingestion and can allegedly produce versions of those works at output, rights treatment is not simply a cleanup task for a legal team after launch. It reaches into how the product is built and what it is allowed to do.

That makes centralized infrastructure a question of control, not just efficiency. When a model developer decides what enters its systems and how those systems handle material later, creators and rightsholders must establish their position from outside that operating environment. The lawsuit does not resolve the legal argument, but it makes the missing layer visible: permissions need to be clear early enough to shape the system, rather than discovered only after a catalogue has been absorbed.

## The context around a work cannot live only with the intermediary

The deeper dependency is that the company seeking to use a work can also control the environment in which the work’s identity and publishing context are handled. Creators should not have to become account holders inside an AI company, distributor, marketplace, or other centralized service simply to establish the context around their work. When that context is fragmented across systems the creator does not operate, permission becomes harder to express before a third party turns a catalogue into product input.

## The argument will move upstream

The Elvis Presley case may turn on facts and legal arguments that remain contested. Its larger lesson is already clear. As AI systems move from general experimentation toward products that can derive commercial value from music, permission will be harder to treat as a separate layer added after technical decisions are made.

Creators, partners, and investors should watch for that shift. The durable question is not merely who can build the most capable model. It is whether the industry builds systems where the people behind the work can establish its context before their catalogues become someone else’s input.
