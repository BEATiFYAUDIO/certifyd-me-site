# Suno’s Apple Music Subpoena Fight Puts Catalog Power at the Center of AI Litigation

Suno is trying to make Apple answer a question that reaches beyond AI training: how dependent is Apple Music on the catalogs of Universal Music Group and Sony Music? In an October 2026 filing, Suno opposed Apple’s effort to block a deposition subpoena in the copyright case brought by UMG and Sony. Apple is not a party to that case and has called the requested testimony irrelevant, overbroad and disproportionate.

The underlying lawsuit alleges that Suno trained its AI on the labels’ recordings without permission. But Suno’s subpoena request also seeks evidence on Apple’s licensing talks with the two majors, streaming fraud on Apple Music, and what the labels have told Apple about Suno. That broadens the argument from how a model was trained to how music reaches listeners, earns attention and affects the economics of a streaming service.

## The dispute is moving downstream

UMG and Sony argue that music created by Suno users is flooding services including Apple Music, eroding market share and diluting royalty pools, according to Suno’s filing. Suno’s response is that Apple Music’s relationship with the major catalogs is relevant to assessing those claims of market harm.

Neither position resolves the case. But the fight makes an uncomfortable industry structure visible: downstream harm is being debated on services whose value and negotiating leverage are deeply tied to catalog licenses held by large rights owners. AI generation, streaming discovery, fraud concerns and royalty-pool arguments may look like separate problems. In practice, they meet inside centralized services that control access to audiences and operate under licensing arrangements creators do not negotiate.

## More than a question of testimony

The immediate issue is whether Apple must provide a witness. The larger question is why so much of music’s operating environment is concentrated in intermediaries that sit between creators, catalogs, listeners and money.

A streaming service can make music easy to find and play while still keeping the essential rules—what is available, how it is ranked, what data is visible and how commercial relationships are structured—inside systems controlled by the service and its licensing partners. When a new technology disrupts that environment, creators and independent rights holders are left watching negotiations among companies that define the market around them.

That is the dependency exposed by Suno’s filing. The argument is not only over whether AI-made music affects streaming. It is over the infrastructure through which that alleged effect becomes measurable, valuable and actionable.

## The case for creator-operated infrastructure

The Suno dispute shows the gap creators face when their work, identity and commercial activity are chiefly mediated by systems owned by streaming services, labels and other intermediaries. The companies at the center of those systems can negotiate catalog access and define the terms of participation, while creators have little infrastructure of their own from which to establish the surrounding context of their work.

## The case will test more than AI training

Apple’s objection may prevail, or Suno may win access to some testimony. The Massachusetts lawsuit itself remains unresolved. Yet the subpoena battle has already sharpened the real industry question: when AI output is said to reshape streaming markets, who controls the systems that determine what those markets are?

For creators, that question matters long before a court answers it. The future of music cannot rest only on debates between models, labels and streaming services. It also depends on whether creators have infrastructure of their own from which to publish, build a public presence and maintain a direct relationship with the activity around their work.
