# AFM’s AI Licensing Fight Puts Performer Terms at the Center of Music’s New Input Economy

The American Federation of Musicians has opposed motions to dismiss its lawsuit involving Universal Music Group, Warner Records and Atlantic Recording Corp. over their licensing deals with AI music companies. In a September 4 filing in New York federal court, the union alleged that recordings made by its members were fed into AI systems for commercial exploitation without payment or credit to those musicians.

The dispute turns on Article 21(a) of the Sound Recording Labor Agreement, a provision addressing a recording’s “new use.” The AFM argues that the provision requires a payment and notice regime when a signatory company uses a covered recording for a purpose not otherwise addressed by the agreement. Universal has argued that no payment is owed because no separate AFM agreement sets a rate for AI use; Warner Records and Atlantic also moved to dismiss.

## AI licensing is becoming a performer-facing question

The immediate issue is contractual interpretation. The larger issue is whether an AI license can be treated solely as a deal between a rights-owning label and an AI company when the commercial value of that deal depends on recorded performances.

That reframes AI licensing. Rather than sitting downstream from a finished release as a conventional exploitation channel, recordings can enter systems that use them as commercial inputs. The question is no longer only who can authorize that entry. It is also whether payment, credit and notice obligations attached to the people on the recording travel with the work into that new use.

The case remains unresolved, but it places older bargaining language in direct contact with a new technical and commercial arrangement. For labels, AI companies and investors, that means the economics of a license may involve contractual allocation questions beyond the license itself.

## The market is moving rights context earlier

Music businesses have often handled performer information, release information and later uses in separate systems. AI licensing puts pressure on that separation. When a recording is repurposed for a new commercial function, the relevant context cannot reliably be reconstructed only after an agreement has been signed or a dispute has emerged.

The deeper shift is from music as a finished asset to music as an input whose context must remain legible across additional uses. Attribution and release history are operational details, but they point to the more fundamental need: creator and work context must persist as recordings move across a growing set of commercial environments.
