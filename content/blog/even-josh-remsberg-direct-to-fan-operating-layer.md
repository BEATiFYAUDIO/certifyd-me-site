# EVEN’s Josh Remsberg Hire Shows Direct-to-Fan Becoming an Operating Layer

EVEN has appointed Josh Remsberg as its new Head of Commercial Partnerships. Remsberg, formerly SVP, Head of Commercial Strategy & Revenue at Warner Records, will oversee EVEN’s relationships with labels, distributors, managers, artist teams, merchandise partners and key industry and chart-reporting organizations.

The appointment matters because the role is new. It signals that EVEN’s direct-to-fan offer is not limited to fan sales. The company is building the commercial relationships that connect an artist-facing service to the wider systems around music releases.

## Direct-to-fan is moving deeper into the business

The obvious reading is straightforward: EVEN is hiring an experienced music-commerce executive as it scales. But the deeper change is about where creator operations happen.

Commerce, partner access and industry reporting have often sat across separate companies and workflows. A direct-to-fan service that brings more of those connections into its own environment can make life easier for an artist team. It can also make that environment more central to how the team reaches partners and participates in the market.

That is a meaningful shift from a simple storefront to an operating layer. The service is no longer only helping creators reach fans; it is becoming a gateway to relationships that shape the commercial life of a release.

## More capability can also mean more dependency

There is nothing unusual about a company building stronger ties with labels, distributors and commerce partners. Those ties can make a service more useful. Yet they also concentrate more of a creator’s working context inside infrastructure the creator does not operate.

The key question is not whether these functions are valuable. They are. It is whether access to fan commerce, partner relationships and release-related context must depend on one centralized intermediary and its rules, agreements and commercial network.

For creators, that question becomes sharper as more business functions move into a single account-based environment. Leaving that environment can mean leaving behind not only a sales tool, but a growing part of the operating context around the artist’s work and audience.

## Control should not stop at the account

This is the gap that makes creator-controlled infrastructure necessary. A service can coordinate partners and commerce, but the creator still receives that access through infrastructure owned and governed by the service. The creator may have more functions in one place while remaining an account within the company’s commercial network.

Certifyd Core takes the opposite direction through its creator-operated infrastructure and network model. Its current positioning is to move supported creator functions away from a centralized intermediary and toward creators operating part of the infrastructure around their work. That changes the control relationship: creator-side infrastructure can become the starting point for the context around an artist’s work, rather than that context existing only inside the service that controls access to partners and commerce.

Certifyd is building toward a broader shift in which creators are not required to place more of their operation inside a single company’s environment just to take part in commercial relationships. For an artist, the practical outcome is a more durable role in operating the infrastructure around their work, rather than relying solely on a third party’s account, rules and business connections.

## A test for the next phase of direct-to-fan

Remsberg’s remit shows that direct-to-fan is becoming part of the music industry’s commercial plumbing. The next question is who controls that plumbing as it grows.

Creators should welcome tools that make commerce and partner coordination easier. They should also watch where their business relationships, fan context and operating records accumulate—and whether they can remain rooted in infrastructure they help operate.
