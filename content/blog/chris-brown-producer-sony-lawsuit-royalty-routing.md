# Chris Brown Producer’s Sony Lawsuit Exposes a Royalty Routing Problem

A royalty interest can be sold as an asset, yet the money can still be stuck behind the administrator’s process. That is the tension in producer Ishmael Sadiq Montague’s lawsuit against Sony Music over royalties from Chris Brown’s 2016 single “Party.”

Music Business Worldwide reports that Montague, who co-produced the track as ISM, filed his complaint in New York federal court on October 16. He alleges Sony delayed redirecting his royalties after he agreed to sell interests including “Party” to Xposure Music Holdings Inc., and that Sony paid him half of the producer royalty rate he was owed. The case remains unresolved.

## The sale was not the whole transaction

Montague’s agreement to sell his royalty interests was reached on June 25, 2026. According to the complaint, payments due from September 1 onward were meant to go to the buyer, but the requested switch was delayed for six weeks.

The dispute is not simply about the price of a catalog asset. It is about the operational gate that sits between an economic agreement and the actual movement of money. Montague alleges that Sony required a letter of direction on its own form, with conditions he objected to, before changing the payee on the account.

That makes a basic point hard to ignore: an income stream may be transferable in a contract, while its practical transfer still depends on the rules, forms and timing of the company controlling payment administration.

## The dependency is built into the route

Labels and other administrators have long handled accounts, statements and payment directions. But when the same intermediary holds the records, defines the process and controls the payment route, creators and buyers are dependent on that intermediary even after they make a deal between themselves.

This is bigger than one complaint or one track. A producer considering a royalty sale needs more than a buyer and a signed agreement. The value of the sale also rests on whether the underlying work and release can be connected to accurate account records and a workable route for changing who is paid.

The market often treats catalog rights as portable assets. In practice, portability is constrained when the operating record and the administrative switch live inside a system the rights holder does not operate.

## A different starting point for creator records

The answer is not merely faster forms. It is reducing the number of creator-business functions that begin and end inside a centralized intermediary’s account system.

Certifyd is building toward creator-owned infrastructure that can support multiple creator-business products from a shared foundation. That direction does not decide the claims in Montague’s case or replace the systems that currently administer royalties. It does challenge the assumption that the essential record of a creator’s catalog must remain concentrated with the intermediary that happens to process payments.

## The asset needs an operating layer

A catalog sale is not fully portable just because an agreement says it is. The operating layer matters: who holds the release context, who recognizes the participant, and who can act when a payment instruction changes.

Montague’s allegations put that dependency in public view. For creators, partners and investors, the lesson is that music rights infrastructure is not only about owning an income stream. It is also about whether the creator has meaningful control over the records and systems that make that stream usable when business relationships change.
