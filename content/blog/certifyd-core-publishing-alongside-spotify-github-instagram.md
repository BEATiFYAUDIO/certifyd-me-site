You can have your music on Spotify, your videos on Instagram and your software on GitHub. Your work can be available almost everywhere people are looking, yet you may still have very little control over the infrastructure supporting your publishing business.

That's a distinction worth exploring, because getting your work in front of people and operating the infrastructure behind that work are two different things. Spotify helps musicians reach listeners, Instagram connects creators with audiences, and GitHub provides developers with tools for collaborating and distributing software. Each serves a valuable purpose, but none of them is necessarily the place where a creator wants every aspect of their publishing operation to live.

This is the thinking behind Certifyd Core. Rather than asking creators to abandon the platforms they're already using, we're building infrastructure that allows them to establish an independent publishing presence alongside those services.

## Distribution is valuable. It just isn't the whole business.

The internet has made distribution remarkably accessible. Independent musicians can reach listeners around the world without manufacturing physical records. Photographers can build audiences and attract customers through social media. Software developers can share applications with people they've never met. These opportunities have fundamentally changed how creative work reaches the public.

But distribution also introduces dependencies that creators don't always recognize until something changes. A platform determines how content is presented, what commercial options are available and how audiences interact with the work. Recommendation systems evolve, account policies change, and features or business models can disappear with relatively little warning.

That doesn't make these services inherently bad. They're businesses operating infrastructure for millions of people, and naturally they make decisions based on their own priorities. The issue is whether creators should depend on those decisions for every part of their own publishing business.

There's nothing wrong with using Spotify to distribute music or Instagram to introduce people to your photography. In fact, those services may be essential to reaching the audience you want. What we're challenging is the assumption that distribution through someone else's infrastructure must also define the boundaries of your entire publishing operation.

## What does it mean to operate your own publishing infrastructure?

For many creators, publishing has become almost synonymous with uploading. You finish a song, photograph, video or application, upload it somewhere, fill in a few fields and click Publish. The service handles the rest, and the creator gets a link to share.

But there's considerably more to publishing than the upload itself. There's the original work, its associated files, the information describing it, the people involved, the credits and the decisions about how and when it becomes available. There's also the public-facing presence through which people discover or access that work.

Certifyd Core approaches these responsibilities as parts of a publishing operation that creators can manage themselves. Core is software that runs on a creator's own computer or server, maintaining work, media and associated records locally. Its private management environment is separated from the public-facing services used to share selected work.

That separation matters because managing a catalogue shouldn't automatically mean exposing every file or record to the public. A creator can organize their work privately and make deliberate decisions about what becomes accessible. Public sharing must be explicitly enabled, rather than being an unavoidable consequence of operating the software.

It's a different relationship with technology. Instead of managing a collection of uploads entirely inside a third-party account, the creator operates the infrastructure responsible for their publishing presence. That comes with additional responsibility, but it also provides an independent layer that doesn't have to replace the services they already rely on.

## Your work can be everywhere and still have a home of its own.

Consider an independent musician preparing a new release. Spotify is an obvious destination because listeners already use it, while Instagram provides somewhere to share performances, announce releases and engage with fans. Neither needs to disappear from the artist's workflow.

The musician might also want a publishing presence connected to their own catalogue, artwork, credits and release information. Through Certifyd Core, they can maintain those records and make selected work available through infrastructure they operate. Spotify remains a destination for listening, while Core provides another place for the creator to organize and publish their work.

The same principle applies to software and photography. A developer might continue using GitHub for source code, collaboration and project history while publishing a finished game or application through their own Certifyd presence. A photographer might continue using Instagram for discovery while maintaining a separate catalogue of work they choose to make publicly available.

These aren't competing workflows. They're different functions that can coexist, giving creators more flexibility over where audiences encounter their work and where the underlying publishing activity takes place.

Importantly, we're talking about the infrastructure used to publish creative work, not suggesting that Certifyd replaces every function of traditional music publishing, rights administration or royalty collection. Those are separate responsibilities with their own legal and commercial requirements.

## A file isn't the same thing as a publication record.

One of the more interesting distinctions in Certifyd Core's beta publishing model is the separation between media files, work information and publication-related records.

An audio file can exist long before a song is released. A photograph may go through several revisions, while a software project might have multiple versions and contributors. Simply possessing the file doesn't necessarily tell you which version was published, what information accompanied it or when the publication took place.

Core maintains these elements as distinct records and uses reproducible cryptographic hashes in its publication model. In practical terms, that provides a consistent reference to the information associated with a particular publication. It's a technical mechanism for recording publishing context, rather than treating the uploaded file as the entire story.

We should also be clear about the limits. A hash doesn't establish legal copyright ownership, resolve a licensing dispute or guarantee that content will remain accessible forever. Publication records aren't a substitute for copyright law or contracts. Their value lies in maintaining clearer references to what was recorded and published, particularly when work exists across multiple services.

For creators working with collaborators, different versions and multiple distribution channels, that distinction is useful. The work itself matters, but the information and relationships surrounding it matter too.

## Independence comes with responsibility.

Operating your own infrastructure means accepting responsibilities that a fully hosted service would otherwise manage. Someone needs to maintain the computer or server running Core, keep the software operational and configure any public connection they want to use.

Certifyd Core supports temporary public connections as well as persistent configurations using a domain and services such as Cloudflare Tunnel. The private administration environment remains separate from the public-facing listener, and enabling a public connection doesn't automatically mean every work is published or ready for commercial access.

This is more involved than signing up for another hosted account, and we shouldn't pretend otherwise. Self-hosting requires some technical understanding and a willingness to manage infrastructure. But for creators who want that option, the additional responsibility is part of the trade-off for greater operational control.

Certifyd Core is currently in full beta, with applications available for Windows, macOS and Linux. The software and its workflows will continue to evolve, but the underlying approach is already tangible: creators can run their own publishing software, maintain their work records and establish a public presence through infrastructure they operate.

## The future doesn't have to be platform versus platform.

A surprising amount of the creator-economy conversation revolves around which platform should replace another. Which streaming service pays better? Which social network offers the best reach? Which marketplace has the most favorable terms?

Those are reasonable questions, but they assume the creator must continue choosing someone else's destination as the center of the business. There's another possibility: distribution and publishing infrastructure don't have to be the same decision.

A musician can use Spotify because that's where people listen. A photographer can use Instagram because that's where people discover visual work. A developer can use GitHub because it's useful for building and collaborating. At the same time, each can establish a publishing presence independent of those services.

That is the direction we're pursuing with Certifyd. Not another centralized platform demanding that everyone move their work into a new ecosystem, but a network built around creators operating their own infrastructure and making their work available on their own terms.

We don't believe creators should have to sacrifice distribution to gain greater independence. They should be able to take advantage of established services while building an independent publishing operation alongside them.

Your work can be everywhere. Your publishing business can still have somewhere to live that you operate yourself.
