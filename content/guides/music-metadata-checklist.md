A release can have the right audio and the wrong records. An artist name points to somebody else. A songwriter credit is missing. Two versions share a title but nobody can identify the master that was delivered.

Keep one release sheet that connects the recording, the composition, the people and the files. Use it when delivering music, registering works and correcting mistakes. Consistent metadata helps those systems match records; it does not establish legal ownership or guarantee a royalty payment.

## Start with one authoritative release sheet

Create a spreadsheet or structured record for each release. Give each recording its own row, keep identifiers as text so leading zeros survive, and record when and where you submitted the data. Keep private contact details and agreements out of public descriptions.

| Check | What to record | What to compare before delivery |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Artist identity | Approved display name, collaborators and the intended artist profile | Spelling, capitalization and the correct person or band |
| Track and release titles | Exact titles and any subtitle or version label | Audio, artwork, distributor entry and public listing |
| Writers and composers | Full credited names; society identifiers if known and applicable | Contributor-confirmed credits and composition records |
| Contributor roles | Performers, producers, remixers and other agreed roles | What each person actually contributed and approved |
| Recording identifier | Assigned ISRC for the particular recording | The code supplied by your label, distributor or ISRC agency |
| Release identifier | UPC/EAN or other release identifier, where required | The actual release package and distributor requirements |
| Rights context | Recording owner, composition owners or administrators, permissions and agreed shares | Signed agreements and the relevant registration records |
| Dates | Recording/version dates, original release date and this release date | Whether this is a first release or a later issue |
| Versions | Studio, live, remix, edit, instrumental or other version | The specific audio and the applicable identifier guidance |
| Source files | Approved master, delivery copy, artwork and credit sheet | Filename, format, duration and version history |

Mark unknown fields as unresolved. Do not fill them with guesses just to complete an upload form.

## Keep artist identity and credits separate

Your artist display name helps listeners recognize you. Songwriter, composer and contributor records explain who did the work. One field cannot safely replace the other.

Confirm names directly with collaborators. Keep any available artist-profile identifiers with your release records, and check the destination profile after delivery. Our coverage of [metadata and mistaken artist identity](/blog/digital-music-news-metadata-royalties-mistaken-identity/) explains why a name alone can be a weak matching key.

## ISRC identifies a recording; it is not a release barcode

Keep the ISRC attached to the recording it identifies. Check for an existing assigned code before requesting another. Releasing an unchanged recording in another package does not automatically make it a new recording. Remixes, live recordings and other changes need a version-specific check against the [IFPI ISRC guidance](https://isrc.ifpi.org/faqs).

A UPC/EAN identifies a release product where the delivery system requires it. Keep the release identifier alongside its track list and recording identifiers; do not substitute it for an ISRC. [GS1 explains product identifiers and barcodes](https://support.gs1.org/support/solutions/articles/43000734095-how-do-gs1-gtins-and-barcodes-work-); check the release requirements of your delivery service as well.

An identifier helps distinguish records. It does not prove that the person entering it owns the recording or composition.

## Record composition rights separately from the master

Keep a clear distinction between the sound recording and the underlying song. Record the relevant owners, publishers or administrators and the permissions that apply to your release. Keep agreed shares and supporting agreements in your private records; confirm disputed information with the people responsible before delivery.

Royalty systems need more than a public title and an audio file. The MLC's [royalty tracking guidance](https://www.themlc.com/royalty-income-tracking) describes registration and recording-to-work matching within its US digital mechanical royalty remit. Other rights, territories and collection routes require their own checks.

Our articles on [QwantumRights and music data](/blog/qwantumrights-music-data-royalties-creator-control/), [Musixmatch and Music Reports](/blog/musixmatch-music-reports-rights-data-operating-layer/) and [unmatched royalties and the MLC](/blog/mlc-2027-black-box-royalty-distribution-market-share/) provide context. They do not replace registration or a review of your agreements.

## Keep an approved master and a correction log

Save the final master separately from working sessions and previews. Use filenames that distinguish versions, and keep a backup of the release sheet, artwork and approved credits. A file hash can help you check whether two copies contain the same bytes; it does not prove who owns them.

For every delivery or correction, record the destination, date, submitted values, confirmation/reference number and result. Compare the live listing with the sheet once the release appears. If you correct a title or credit, check the other systems that received the older values.

## Before you release: a five-minute consistency pass

- Confirm the artist profile, exact titles and version labels.
- Compare the ISRC and release identifier with the original assignment records.
- Ask collaborators to confirm names, roles and unresolved credits.
- Check the approved audio, artwork, dates and delivery requirements.
- Save the submitted record and check the resulting public page.
