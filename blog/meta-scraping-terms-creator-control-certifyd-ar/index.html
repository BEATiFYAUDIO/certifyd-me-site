Meta’s Scraping Terms Show Why Creators Need Control Beyond Social Media

Meta’s proposed scraping terms have publishers asking who really controls their content. Certifyd Core and Certifyd AR offer a different approach: creator-operated identity and cryptographic verification that augment the existing web.

By CertifydPublished October 23, 2026Updated October 23, 2026
MetaNews/Media Allianceweb scrapingpublisher rightscreator infrastructuredirect commerce

Imagine signing up for a social media business account, only to discover that the terms could give the company permission to collect material from your own website.

Not just the photos, videos or articles you've posted on its platform. Content you've published somewhere else, on infrastructure you operate yourself.

That's the concern behind a dispute between Meta and the News/Media Alliance, which represents more than 2,000 American news publishers.

The association is asking Meta to suspend commercial terms scheduled to take effect on October 30, 2026. According to reporting by Nieman Journalism Lab, the terms could permit mass scraping of websites associated with business accounts across Facebook, Instagram, WhatsApp and Threads.

The publishers say there is no meaningful opt-out under the proposed arrangement.

This isn't simply another disagreement over social media policies. It raises a bigger question about the modern internet: how much control should a platform gain over your independent work simply because you use its services?

And what would the internet look like if creators could establish their identity and operate their businesses without depending on those platforms in the first place?

When a business account becomes a permission slip

Publishers use Meta's services for good reasons. Facebook and Instagram can help them reach audiences, distribute stories, promote subscriptions and attract advertisers.

But those benefits come with terms set by Meta.

In a letter to CEO Mark Zuckerberg, News/Media Alliance president and CEO Danielle Coffey asked the company to withdraw the proposed terms and work with publishers on a more transparent arrangement.

The association wants clarity about which Meta products would use the collected material and for what purposes. It also wants separate permission for scraping, identification of the crawlers involved, and respect for website controls such as robots.txt files, registration walls and paywalls.

Those requests aren't particularly radical. Publishers want to know what is being collected from their websites, why it's being collected and whether they can say no.

Meta removed the word "train" from the terms on October 16 and said future use of scraped content would be limited to advertising and business products. However, legal experts cited by Nieman Journalism Lab said the revised wording could still allow broad scraping.

The concern remains: participation in a social media business service could potentially carry consequences for content hosted outside that service.

Your website should not become somebody else's resource

There's an important difference between uploading content to Facebook and operating an independent website.

When a publisher uploads something to a social network, it enters an environment governed by that network's terms. The publisher may retain copyright, but the service generally receives certain permissions to host and use the uploaded material.

An independent website is different. The publisher operates that space, chooses how its content is presented and can establish its own access rules.

The reported Meta terms raise concerns because they could blur that boundary.

A company that provides social media accounts, advertising tools and audience access may also seek contractual permission to collect material from websites that it doesn't operate.

For publishers, that's a serious dependency problem. The more their audience relationships and commercial activity rely on one company, the harder it becomes to reject terms that reach beyond the platform itself.

And this isn't limited to newspapers.

Musicians, filmmakers, podcasters, writers and other independent creators face versions of the same problem. Their work may originate elsewhere, but the accounts they depend on for discovery and audience access are controlled by companies whose policies can change.

Owning your content isn't the same as controlling the infrastructure around it.

The real problem is who controls your digital identity

Consider how most creators exist online today.

A musician has a Spotify profile, a YouTube channel, an Instagram account and perhaps a TikTok following. Each service maintains its own version of the creator's identity, audience relationships and activity.

Those accounts can be valuable, but they're not the same as an independent digital identity.

If a platform changes its rules, restricts access or disappears, the creator may lose an important connection to the people who found them there.

The traditional response is to build a presence on more platforms. That can reduce dependence on any single service, but it doesn't change the underlying architecture.

The creator is still operating through accounts maintained by other companies.

At Certifyd, we think the starting point should be different.

The creator should establish the foundation. Applications should connect around it.

That's the idea behind Certifyd Core, and it's also why we built Certifyd AR.

Certifyd AR: Augmenting Web2 instead of replacing it

What if creators didn't have to abandon the existing internet to gain more control over their digital identity?

That's the idea behind Certifyd AR, our approach to augmenting Web2 with creator-controlled identity and verification.

When most people hear "augmented reality," they imagine pointing a phone camera at something and seeing digital objects appear over the physical world.

Certifyd AR applies a similar concept to the web itself.

Through the Certifyd browser extension and the Certifyd AR Browser, supported websites can be augmented with additional identity information and creator-configured interactions.

The original website remains intact. Certifyd adds another layer to the browsing experience.

A fan might discover an artist on a familiar website, while Certifyd AR provides a way to check whether that account is connected to an independently established Certifyd identity.

That's a different approach from asking the artist to move everything into another platform.

We're not trying to replace the internet people already use. We're making it possible to bring creator-operated identity into it.

How Certifyd AR actually works

The technology begins with Certifyd Core, the desktop software creators operate on their own infrastructure.

Core provides the foundation for a creator's identity, work and published records. Creators can also establish connections between their Certifyd identity and accounts they use elsewhere on the internet.

Certifyd AR uses those relationships to augment supported browsing experiences.

Recognizing a creator across the web

When someone visits a supported Web2 profile, the Certifyd extension can identify the account being viewed and look for a corresponding Certifyd identity.

That connection isn't supposed to depend on a matching username or profile picture alone.

The extension can check published account proofs against the creator's Core identity, establishing whether the account being viewed has a supported, verifiable relationship to that creator.

This matters because a familiar name or recognizable image isn't necessarily reliable evidence of identity, particularly as impersonation and AI-generated content become easier to produce.

Cryptographic verification, not just another badge

Certifyd AR also supports cryptographically signed records used to authorize augmented experiences.

A digital signature allows the browser to check whether a record has been altered and whether it was signed by a key authorized through the creator's identity.

The verification process can connect several pieces of information: the Web2 account being viewed, its relationship to a Certifyd identity, the signed AR record and the authority of the key behind that record.

When the required checks succeed, the extension can display supported creator-configured information or actions alongside the existing page.

This is more meaningful than simply placing a decorative badge next to someone's name.

The browser can verify a technical relationship rather than asking the visitor to trust an unsupported visual claim.

That doesn't automatically establish legal ownership of a recording, prove every statement about a person or guarantee that an account is trustworthy. It provides verifiable evidence of specific identity relationships and authorizations.

Those distinctions are important.

The augmentation happens in the browser

There's another part of the technology worth understanding.

Certifyd AR doesn't require Spotify, YouTube or another supported website to replace its own account system with Certifyd.

The augmentation happens in the participating user's browser.

The website continues providing its normal experience, while the Certifyd extension can add supported information and interactions connected to the creator's independent identity.

This doesn't override the website's rules, change its underlying database or prevent it from exercising its own policies. Support also depends on the website and the browser integration.

But it demonstrates a different architectural possibility.

A creator can participate in an existing online service without requiring that service to become the sole authority over the creator's digital identity.

Why cryptographic identity matters in the AI era

The internet is changing quickly.

AI-generated voices, images, music and video are becoming more convincing. Automated accounts can imitate human activity, and familiar names or images can be copied into convincing-looking profiles.

For creators, that makes identity increasingly important.

If someone encounters a musician's profile, how can they establish whether it's actually connected to that musician's independent identity?

A platform verification badge can provide useful information, but the platform itself decides how that badge is issued and maintained.

Certifyd approaches the problem from another direction.

By allowing supported account relationships and AR records to be checked cryptographically against a creator-operated identity, Certifyd provides another technical basis for verification.

The creator's identity doesn't have to begin as a badge granted by the website where someone happens to encounter them.

It can originate through infrastructure the creator operates.

And that becomes especially interesting when the same identity connects multiple accounts, works and published records.

The result isn't a universal guarantee of authenticity. It's a way to make specific claims about identity and authorization independently checkable.

As more of the web becomes synthetic, that distinction is going to matter.

Certifyd Core, AR and Fan: Three parts of one architecture

Certifyd isn't simply building another creator website.

We're building an ecosystem in which creators can operate their own infrastructure while fans and applications interact with that infrastructure through different experiences.

Certifyd Core is the foundation. It runs on the creator's computer and provides the creator-operated environment for identity, catalog records, publishing and supported commerce.

Certifyd AR connects that foundation to Web2. Through supported browser integrations, it can recognize linked accounts, verify authorized records and augment existing websites with Certifyd-connected information and actions.

Certifyd Fan provides discovery and playback. Fans can explore creators and their work through a dedicated experience without needing to understand how the underlying infrastructure operates.

These aren't three competing destinations. They serve different roles in the same architecture.

Core gives creators an operating foundation. AR brings supported identity relationships into existing browsing experiences. Fan gives audiences another way to discover and engage with the network.

That separation is important because no single application needs to become the permanent home of every creator's digital existence.

Commerce shouldn't depend on a social media account

The Meta dispute is about scraping permissions, not directly about music sales or fan purchases. But it exposes the same underlying issue that affects creator commerce.

When a social network becomes the primary gateway to an audience, the company controlling that gateway can set the conditions for participation.

That can affect much more than posting content. It can influence discovery, commercial relationships and how creators reach the people who support their work.

Certifyd takes a different approach.

Through Certifyd Core, creators can operate their own publishing infrastructure and offer supported direct-commerce experiences. Where enabled, fans can purchase or unlock content using Bitcoin and Lightning.

The creator can set the offer and price, while the payment and access workflow operates through their Core configuration.

That doesn't mean creators should stop using social media. Instagram, Facebook, YouTube and other services can remain useful ways to reach people.

But a creator's ability to publish work and establish a direct commercial relationship shouldn't have to begin with permission from a social media account provider.

The social network can be a discovery channel.

It doesn't have to be the business.

The internet doesn't need another company in the middle

The News/Media Alliance is asking Meta for something straightforward: publishers want meaningful control over whether their independently operated websites can be scraped under the terms of a separate business-account relationship.

The dispute is a reminder that the boundary between a platform and the people using it matters.

A creator can own a website, control the files on it and retain copyright while still depending heavily on outside companies for identity, discovery and commercial access.

That's the dependency Certifyd is working to reduce.

Certifyd Core gives creators infrastructure they operate. Certifyd AR extends supported identity and verification experiences into the existing web. Certifyd Fan gives audiences a way to discover and experience the network.

Together, they offer a different starting point: creators establish their own foundation, and applications connect around them.

We're not arguing that every existing platform should disappear tomorrow. We're demonstrating that creators don't have to build their entire digital existence inside those platforms for the internet to work.

The web can remain familiar. Discovery can remain convenient. Applications can continue competing to provide better experiences.

But the creator should not have to surrender the foundation underneath their identity and work simply to participate.

That's the future Certifyd is building toward.

Real fans. Real artists. Real value.
