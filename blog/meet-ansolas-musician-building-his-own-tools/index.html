Meet Ansolas: The Musician Who Started Building His Own Tools

Musician and builder Ansolas is turning problems from his own creative workflow into software, using AI to expand what he can build and operate, and experimenting with new ways to control his digital infrastructure.

Meet Ansolas: The Musician Who Started Building His Own Tools

We've known Ansolas for years. He's a musician, builder, collaborator and friend, and one of those people who doesn't seem particularly interested in waiting around for somebody else to build the thing he needs. His latest project, Logic Loop Manager, is a perfect example.

It didn't start with a startup pitch or a search for a product category. It started with an irritating problem Ansolas kept encountering while making music in Logic Pro. Eventually, after dealing with it enough times, he decided to fix it himself.

It Started With Apple Loops

Ansolas creates a lot of Apple Loops while working in Logic Pro. Logic makes the initial process remarkably convenient: take a musical idea, turn it into a loop, add the relevant information and put it into the Loop Browser. For someone creating regularly, it can become a useful library of musical ideas accumulated over time.

The frustration comes when something needs to change. Once Ansolas had created a loop and added it to his library, he found there wasn't a convenient way to go back and change its metadata. Correcting something as basic as the key or tempo could mean returning to the original material, importing it again, entering the information again and creating another Apple Loop, while the previous version remained in the library.

Do that often enough and a library designed to make musical ideas easier to find gradually becomes harder to maintain. A tiny correction can turn into an unnecessarily cumbersome process of finding the original, loading it again, tagging it again, recreating the loop and dealing with the old version.

Ansolas didn't want to replace Logic's Loop Browser; he actually likes using it. What he wanted was control over the library behind it, so he started building the missing piece himself.

Building Logic Loop Manager

Logic Loop Manager is designed to let musicians work more directly with the Apple Loops they've already created. The goal is to review existing loops, correct metadata, change information such as key or tempo, organize the library and deal with unwanted entries without recreating the original Apple Loop every time.

It's a focused piece of software because it came from a focused problem. Ansolas wasn't sitting around trying to think of an app he could build; he was making music, encountered the same limitation repeatedly and eventually reached the conclusion familiar to creators everywhere: there has to be a better way to do this.

Some of the most useful creative tools come from people who actually live inside the workflow they're trying to improve. They understand the little problems that might look insignificant from the outside but become maddening when encountered every day. Logic Loop Manager is one of those projects.

The application is currently being tested with a deliberately small group of real users. Ansolas knows that something behaving perfectly on the computer where it was developed doesn't mean it will behave the same way on somebody else's machine. Different libraries, systems and workflows expose different problems, and he'd rather find and understand those problems now than discover them after putting the software in front of a much larger audience.

A Musician Building Software With AI

There's another part of Ansolas's story that makes what he's doing particularly interesting in 2026: he's using Claude Code as part of his development process. AI is helping him cross some of the technical distance between understanding exactly what a tool should do and actually creating that tool.

That distinction matters. Ansolas already has the domain expertise because he's the musician experiencing the problem. He understands the workflow, knows where the friction occurs and has a clear idea of how he'd rather work. Claude Code gives him another way to translate that knowledge into working software.

For decades, musicians encountering software limitations generally had a few choices. They could develop a workaround, search for a plug-in, learn to program themselves or hope the company behind the software eventually addressed the problem. AI-assisted development is beginning to add another option: the person with the problem can participate much more directly in building the solution.

Logic Loop Manager is an example of that shift. Instead of simply saying, "I wish somebody would make this," Ansolas can increasingly say, "I'm building it."

The Same Philosophy Goes Beyond the Studio

That builder mentality doesn't stop with Logic Loop Manager. Ansolas has also been experimenting with what happens when a musician operates more of the infrastructure behind their digital work themselves.

He's currently running a sovereign creator node using Certifyd Core. From his own system, he's publishing into the network, collaborating with other participants and taking part in real transactions. He's already earning satoshis through activity on the network while helping test how independently operated creator infrastructure behaves outside a development environment.

For Ansolas, it's another expression of the same instinct behind Logic Loop Manager. One project gives him more control over a frustrating part of his music-production workflow; the other lets him experiment with greater control over the infrastructure surrounding his work, identity and relationships online.

What makes his setup especially interesting is that Claude Code is involved here too. Ansolas isn't only using AI to help him build software; he's also using it to help understand, manage and operate the computer running his creator node.

AI as an Operator, Not Just a Coding Tool

Running your own infrastructure traditionally comes with an unpleasant condition: somebody has to know how to operate it. Software needs configuration and maintenance, services occasionally fail, networking can become complicated and cryptic error messages need to be understood. For most musicians, becoming a part-time system administrator is understandably not high on the list of reasons they started making music.

Ansolas is experimenting with a different relationship. Claude Code can help him understand what's happening on his system, work through technical problems, interact with the software and manage tasks that would otherwise require considerably more specialized knowledge. He's still the person deciding what he wants the machine to do, but AI can help him deal with the machinery underneath those decisions.

That makes his use of AI more interesting than the familiar story about generating content. He's using it to expand his capabilities: first as a builder creating a tool around his own musical workflow, and then as an operator managing infrastructure he controls.

For independent creators, that combination could matter. The barrier to owning more of your digital infrastructure has never only been the cost of the hardware or availability of the software; complexity has been a major obstacle too. AI doesn't eliminate that complexity, but people like Ansolas are beginning to show how much of it can be made approachable.

The Musician Is Becoming the Builder

The common thread through everything Ansolas is doing isn't AI, Bitcoin, Certifyd or even Logic Loop Manager. It's agency. When he finds a problem in his creative workflow, he's increasingly able to build something to address it rather than simply accepting it.

That same approach now extends beyond making software. He's operating his own creator infrastructure, publishing his work into a network, collaborating with other independent participants and receiving value in satoshis through that activity. These are experiments, and both Logic Loop Manager and the infrastructure he's running are still evolving, but that's precisely what makes his work interesting.

None of these things individually represents some definitive future of music. Taken together, though, they show how dramatically the toolbox available to an independent musician is changing. A creator can make the work, identify problems in the tools, build new software around those problems and increasingly operate sophisticated infrastructure without needing a separate company or technical team for every step.

Musicians Have Always Been Technologists

There's something familiar about all of this. Musicians have always pushed technology in directions its designers didn't anticipate, modifying instruments, abusing recording equipment, turning turntables into instruments and finding creative uses for machines that weren't necessarily designed for them.

Home recording followed a similar path. Technology that once required enormous studios gradually became accessible enough that musicians could own and operate increasingly sophisticated production environments themselves. Entire careers can now be built from rooms containing equipment that would have seemed impossibly powerful a generation ago.

Digital software gave musicians extraordinary new capabilities, but it also created environments that creators often couldn't substantially modify themselves. AI may be shifting that balance again by giving people with highly specific creative knowledge greater ability to shape the software around their own needs.

That's what makes Ansolas interesting to us. He's not abandoning being a musician to become a software developer or system administrator. He's still a musician; he just has a much bigger toolbox.

Build What You Wish Existed

Logic Loop Manager is still being tested, and the creator infrastructure Ansolas is operating is still evolving. He's finding problems, learning from other users, testing what works and discovering what needs to improve rather than waiting until everything is polished, packaged and obvious.

We've watched that builder instinct in Ansolas for years, and it's exciting to see where he's taking it now. He's making music, building Logic Loop Manager, using Claude Code to extend what he can do, operating his own creator node, publishing work, collaborating with other people and earning sats along the way.

The technology is interesting, but the person using it is the better story. Ansolas isn't waiting for somebody else to decide what tools a musician should have or what an independent creator should be capable of doing.

He's building what he wishes existed.
