QwantumRights Solutions: What Your Music Data Doesn’t Know Could Be Costing You Money

A Digital Music News partner article from QwantumRights Solutions argues that incomplete rights and repertoire data can turn into delayed, misallocated or missed royalties. The deeper issue is who controls the records that connect a work to payment.

By CertifydPublished October 7, 2026Updated October 7, 2026
music rightsmusic metadataroyaltiescreator infrastructuremusic businessCertifyd

QwantumRights Solutions argues in a Digital Music News partner article that weak music data is not merely an administrative headache. When repertoire and rights information is incomplete, inconsistent or poorly reconciled, royalties can be delayed, misallocated or missed. The article’s central warning is simple: regular royalty statements and established industry relationships do not prove that the underlying records are correct.

A payment problem that starts much earlier

The obvious lesson is that creators and rights holders need accurate metadata. But the harder problem is that a work can pass through writers, publishers, collective management organizations, administrators, labels and digital services, each with records that may not match. A payment shortfall may therefore begin long before a statement arrives, in an ownership share, creator identification or conflicting version of a work.

QwantumRights Solutions describes an engagement in which a review found thousands of incorrect repertoire entries, including misidentified creators and publishers, incorrect ownership shares and conflicting work versions. According to the article, correcting the data and process flows produced measurable revenue uplift. The point is not that every discrepancy has the same result; it is that small errors can accumulate across a catalogue until they become a material collection problem.

The deeper dependency is control of the record

Metadata checks are necessary, but they do not answer the larger question: why must the records that shape a creator’s economic life live mainly across systems the creator does not operate? The music business has added more sophisticated databases, standards and reporting layers, yet creators can still be asked to trust that others have carried their work, identity and ownership context forward correctly.

That is an intermediary-operated model, even when it involves many organizations rather than one company. It can improve administration, but the creator remains dependent on outside rules, relationships and reconciliation processes to see whether the record behind a payment is complete. Better downstream review helps; a stronger starting point is for creators to hold a real role in the infrastructure where their identity and publishing context begin.

Records should not begin and end inside someone else’s system

This is the gap Certifyd addresses at an architectural level. If essential creator identity and publishing context originate only inside administrator, rights-management or service databases, creators remain accounts inside systems that set the rules for how that context is held and carried forward. When records diverge, the creator often has to discover the issue after the fact and work back through those systems to repair it.

Certifyd exists to reduce dependency on centralized platforms by giving creator identity, publishing context, commerce activity and public records a stronger creator-controlled foundation. Its public network entry, node registration and profile surfaces are live today. The broader direction is a network where supported creator functions move away from being controlled solely by an intermediary and toward infrastructure creators operate themselves. In this story, that changes the creator’s position: rather than relying entirely on outside systems to preserve the context behind a work, the creator can take part in the record layer from which that context originates.

Treat data maintenance as revenue protection

For creators, the practical takeaway is to treat repertoire, ownership and contributor information as live commercial records. Check that work and rights details agree across the services involved in administration and collection, and revisit them as relationships, releases and catalogues change. Waiting for a visible royalty gap can mean discovering a problem after it has spread.

For partners and investors, the signal is equally clear. The next layer of music infrastructure is not just better reporting after money moves. It is infrastructure that gives creators a stronger role in the records that determine whether value can be identified, attributed and collected in the first place.
