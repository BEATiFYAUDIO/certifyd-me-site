Spotify, Amazon Music and MFFA Push for Prison Time in Michael Smith Streaming Fraud Case

Ahead of Michael Smith’s October 6 sentencing, the Music Fights Fraud Alliance has urged a significant prison sentence in a case centered on bot-assisted streams and diverted royalties.

By CertifydPublished October 3, 2026Updated October 3, 2026
streaming fraudSpotifyAmazon MusicMusic Fights Fraud Alliancecreator infrastructuremusic royalties

Spotify, Amazon Music, DistroKid, Downtown, EMPIRE, Merlin, Meta and other Music Fights Fraud Alliance members have urged a significant sentence for Michael Smith ahead of his October 6 sentencing in New York. Smith was convicted of stealing royalties from artists and songwriters through millions of bot-assisted streams on AI-generated content.

The alliance’s letter to Judge John G. Koeltl argues that the sentence should show the public that streaming fraud has real legal consequences. Digital Music News describes the case as the first criminal prosecution for streaming fraud in the United States.

Fraud is a royalty problem, not just a bad metric

The immediate point is clear: fake listening can redirect money away from the people whose music earned it. The MFFA says more than $8 million in royalties were stolen at the expense of creators whose payments were siphoned away.

But the case also shows the limits of treating fraud only as an internal moderation or payment issue. The response comes from a coalition spanning streaming services, distributors, publishers, collection organizations and other music businesses. That is a sign that bot-assisted activity is being treated as harm across the music economy, not merely as an individual service’s data-quality problem.

For creators, the important distinction is between attention that reflects a real audience and activity manufactured to trigger payments. A music business built around counts must be able to defend which counts represent real customer activity.

The control question behind enforcement

Collective enforcement may deter abuse, but it also highlights where control sits today. Detection, decisions about suspicious activity, and the systems through which music participation is measured are concentrated within companies and industry bodies. Creators are often affected by those decisions without operating the underlying environment.

That concentration becomes more important as more of a creator’s working life is pulled into intermediary systems: release context, audience access, discovery and promotion. A creator can gain useful services, yet still depend on rules, records and relationships they do not control.

The deeper question is not whether fraud controls are needed. They are. It is whether the basic context around a work and release must remain fragmented across centralized services for the industry to establish what is real and who should benefit.

The Smith case makes the cost of getting that distinction wrong unusually visible.

A criminal case, and an unfinished infrastructure problem

A substantial sentence would not by itself settle how the music business should prevent bot-driven royalty diversion. It would, however, establish a sharper consequence for conduct that the MFFA says harms artists, songwriters and the wider digital music ecosystem.

The longer-term task is broader than enforcement. Music needs systems that make it harder for manufactured activity to compete with real listener relationships—and that leave creators with more control over the context surrounding the work they make.
