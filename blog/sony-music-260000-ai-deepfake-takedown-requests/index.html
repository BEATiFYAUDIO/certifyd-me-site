Sony Music’s 260,000 AI Deepfake Requests Show Why Takedowns Cannot Be the Whole System

Sony Music says it has asked digital platforms to remove more than 260,000 AI-generated tracks impersonating its artists. The scale turns AI voice imitation from an isolated rights dispute into a recurring infrastructure problem.

By CertifydPublished October 5, 2026Updated October 5, 2026
AI deepfakesSony Musicartist identitymusic rightscreator infrastructure

Sony Music has asked digital platforms to remove more than 260,000 AI-generated tracks impersonating artists on its roster, including Harry Styles, Adele and Michael Jackson. The reported total is almost double the 135,000 tracks Sony had sought to remove by the end of March.

Sony told the Financial Times that the work is becoming an uphill struggle. It expects platforms to act quickly and prevent the same material from returning. The report did not say how many tracks were removed, how fast services acted, or which services received the requests.

A recurring operating burden

The immediate issue is unauthorized voice and likeness imitation. But the reported volume points to a larger problem: enforcement is no longer an occasional dispute handled one track at a time. It is repeated work—finding impersonations, submitting requests and watching for reuploads.

The figures suggest more than 20,000 AI-deepfake takedown requests per month across April through September. At that scale, removal systems remain reactive. The disputed track has already entered a service and created another enforcement task for the artist or rights holder.

The control problem starts before removal

A takedown process puts the decisive workflow inside the service hosting the disputed material. Reporting routes, review processes, response times and efforts to stop repeat uploads are controlled by infrastructure the creator does not operate.

That may address a report, but it leaves a basic question unanswered: why should an artist’s public identity and publishing context first need to be interpreted inside a centralized intermediary when a fake appears? The need is not just for faster complaints. It is for creator-originated context that can establish a clearer starting point for authentic activity before an imitation becomes a dispute.

For creators, that makes identity and publishing context less dependent on a single intermediary’s reporting and review system. From volume to trust

Sony’s reported tally does not show that one technical or policy response will solve AI impersonation. It does show that a system based mainly on after-the-fact removal is under strain.

As imitation becomes easier to produce at scale, authentic creator identity cannot remain an afterthought attached to distribution and discovery systems. Creator-operated network infrastructure will matter more when artists need a durable basis for presenting their own public context before a disputed track reaches another takedown queue.
