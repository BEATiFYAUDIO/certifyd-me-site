Elvis and Eminem Rights Holders Sue Suno, Putting AI Music Permissions Inside the Product

New lawsuits against Suno over alleged AI music and lyric mimicry show why permissions and rights context cannot remain an after-the-fact policy problem.

By CertifydPublished November 9, 2026Updated November 9, 2026
AI musicSunoElvis PresleyEminemmusic rightsartist likenesspermissionsrights context

Elvis Presley and Eminem are now part of a much more concrete fight over AI music. The Verge reported on November 8, 2026, that a group of Elvis Presley music rights holders and the publisher of some of Eminem’s biggest records are suing Suno, alleging that the company generates music and lyrics that mimic the artists.

The Presley-related suit alleges a violation of Tennessee’s Ensuring Likeness Voice and Image Security (ELVIS) Act. Suno says it is built to help people make new, original music; it says it prohibits unauthorized use of artists’ work, blocks prompts for specific artists’ names and songs, and uses third-party screening for uploaded audio and lyrics. Those competing positions put the real product question in view: a guardrail is not the same thing as a clear, operable boundary around permission.

Permission can no longer sit outside the product

The immediate story is another pair of lawsuits for Suno. The larger shift is that authorization is moving from a policy and contract issue handled around a product into something AI music products must address in the course of creation itself.

That matters because a prompt, an uploaded file and an output can each carry different creative signals. If an output is alleged to imitate a recognizable artist’s voice, music or lyrics, the relevant question is not limited to whether a user typed an artist’s name. Products will increasingly be judged by the relationship between the safeguards they describe and the results they enable.

For creators and rights holders, this raises the bar for knowing what work is involved, who contributed to it, what material is being referenced and what authorization exists. Rights clearance is not simply an administrative step after value has been created. In AI music, it is becoming part of the boundary that needs to exist before and during creation.

Work context needs to be clear

Clear catalog context will not settle a legal dispute on its own. But disconnected records make review harder when questions arise about source material, contributors and publication history.

Certifyd’s approach starts from a different control point: creator identity and publishing context should have a stronger creator-controlled foundation, rather than living only inside centralized services. In its beta catalog capability, Certifyd Core keeps work metadata, media-file references, external identifiers, source references and contributor credits as separate records. Its publication helper can also bind work metadata to publication context through a reproducible canonical manifest hash.

That gives creators a way to maintain the context around a work as part of their own operating record. A reference to outside material is still not proof of copyright ownership or a transfer of rights. But separating references, local works and credits makes a more legible starting point than leaving critical context scattered across prompts, uploads and third-party product databases.

The control question behind the lawsuits

The lawsuits do not resolve how AI music permissions should work across the industry. They do show that the old assumption—that rights questions can be handled mainly through terms, moderation and a complaint after release—is under strain.

When creative identity can be invoked or allegedly reproduced inside a generative product, permission needs a place in the product architecture. Creator-controlled records cannot replace every rights system overnight, and Certifyd’s catalog capability is currently in beta. But giving creators a durable way to hold work, credit and publication context moves an important part of the operating record out of a centralized intermediary’s black box.

That is the practical demand underneath this dispute: not just better AI guardrails, but clearer authorization and work context that can stay connected to the work before questions turn into conflict.
