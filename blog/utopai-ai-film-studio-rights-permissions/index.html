Utopai’s $1bn Valuation Puts AI Film Rights at the Center of the Studio Model

Utopai Studios has raised $60m at a $1bn valuation. The bigger story is what happens when a film studio owns both the production agenda and the AI infrastructure behind it.

By CertifydPublished October 28, 2026Updated October 28, 2026
Utopai StudiosAI filmcreator rightspermissionsrights clearancecreative infrastructure

Utopai Studios has raised $60m at a $1bn valuation, according to a Music Ally report published on October 26, 2026. The company has more than 20 projects in development, including three films and two TV shows. That is a striking vote of confidence in an AI-native film business—but the more important question is what kind of studio is being funded.

Utopai is not presented simply as a production company using off-the-shelf AI tools. The report says its valuation may partly reflect its use of its own AI models, and notes that the company began as Cyberver, building a model for complex 3D environments. The studio’s development slate and its production technology are becoming part of the same business.

The studio is becoming the system

The immediate reading is that investors see productions that traditional economics may not support. Utopai CEO Cecilia Shen has said the company is not trying to make cheaper movies, but movies that traditional economics might not allow. That is a meaningful creative ambition.

The structural change sits underneath it. When a studio owns the generative system used to develop work, the competitive asset is no longer only the individual film or show. It is also the production environment: the models, the operating rules, the creative inputs and the decisions made inside that environment.

That makes the rights question more urgent. Permissions, contributor terms and context around source material cannot remain a paperwork problem considered after creative work is underway. In AI-led production, those questions need to be clear early enough to follow the work through development.

Permissions cannot live only inside the production system

A proprietary production system may create more room for ambitious work. It also concentrates more of the creative operation inside an intermediary-controlled environment: where work is developed, how participation is governed, and which records are visible to the people involved.

Creators, rights holders and production partners should not have to rely solely on a studio, tool provider or distribution intermediary to hold the public context around their identity and publishing activity. The question is not whether a studio should give up control of its tools. It is whether a creator’s presence in a project must exist only as an account and set of records inside a company-owned system.

That is the AI-era permissions challenge. Clear agreements still matter, but the market also needs infrastructure that gives creators a basis for identity and public creative context outside the systems that temporarily organize a production.

A different control relationship

Certifyd exists to reduce creator dependency on centralized platforms by giving creator identity, publishing context, commerce activity and public records a stronger creator-controlled foundation. That distinction matters when the same company can increasingly control the production environment, its generative technology and the operating context around a project.

Certifyd’s public network includes a Join Network path, a Register Node page and a live public profile link. These are current public surfaces. They point to a different direction from the studio-operated model: supported creator functions can move toward infrastructure that creators participate in and operate, rather than remaining entirely inside the company that runs the production environment.

For a creator or production partner, the practical issue is whether their identity and public creative context have any footing beyond the studio system that organized a particular project. Utopai’s funding is a reminder that the race is not merely to build better generative tools. It is also to decide who controls the infrastructure around the work those tools help make.

The next value question

Utopai’s valuation suggests investors see proprietary AI infrastructure as a core studio asset. The unanswered industry question is whether the surrounding permission and rights context will be treated with the same seriousness.

As studios combine technology ownership with production, creators will need more than access to new tools. They will need a position in infrastructure that does not make their creative presence dependent on a single company’s operating environment.
